## Introduction

Copying is routine in the tech industry. Yet there is one UI element that Apple and Microsoft have never borrowed from each other: the macOS Dock and the Windows taskbar. For thirty years, neither company has copied the other's version. That is not an accident. It comes from two different answers to a basic question — what is a computer actually for? As of today, the two answers still have nothing in common.

This piece is about **how Microsoft used verbs to disarm users' fear, how Apple focused on tangible objects, and why the two companies could never truly copy each other**.

## 1990s

Go back to the **1990s**. Microsoft and Apple, the two giants, were both standing at a cliff edge, and market pressure was forcing each of them to redefine its product.

In **Windows before 1995**, the core interface — **Program Manager** — was fundamentally broken for what users needed.

<PostFigure
  src={figure2}
  alt="The Windows for Workgroups 3.11 desktop with Program Manager open as a window"
  caption="Unknown author. Windows for Workgroups 3.11 desktop. Self-taken screenshot. Fair use."
/>

In **Windows 3.1**, the **application list** was just another window. You could minimise it, lose it among a dozen other windows, or close it by mistake and find yourself staring at **an empty desktop**. It was almost like **closing the Home Screen on an iPhone**.

<PostFigure
  src={figure3}
  alt="An iOS 26 iPhone Home Screen"
  caption="DabAZ. iOS 26 interface. Modified self-taken screenshot."
/>

Microsoft's **internal research** <sup>[1]</sup> found that many users were confused when they tried to run two apps at once. **Minimising** windows and **overlapping** them was not intuitive, so beginners kept opening the same app several times until the **system overloaded and crashed**. That ran directly against **Windows' core promise**: **multitasking**.

The **taskbar**, the UI element that would later become central to the graphical interface, did not exist yet. So although **Windows** dominated the PC desktop, a good share of its users were still confused and frustrated by **window management**. The question was: **how would Microsoft get out of this?** Microsoft's usual move was to find a solution another company had already built and copy it — and that company was usually **Apple**. Not this time. **macOS** was in worse shape: similar **UI problems**, and roughly **ten times** less stable.

So why didn't the two companies simply borrow from each other? The answer is simple — **there was nothing to borrow**. Both had to find their own way out.

Microsoft started testing **"Chicago"** <sup>[1]</sup>, the project that became **Windows 95**.

At the same time, Apple, on the edge of collapse, bought NeXT — not only to get the more stable NeXTSTEP operating system, but to bring back its most important asset, Steve Jobs. And NeXTSTEP already contained a solution, in a different form.

At the same moment, two struggling companies faced the same question — **"Where do I start?"** — and gave completely different answers.

When the behavioural scientist Daniel Oran joined Microsoft, nobody expected him to shape the company's design philosophy for decades. He and the "Chicago" team realised the problem was not technical. It was psychological.

Users were afraid of computers. They worried they would "break" the machine, and in those days they often did. The technical fix was easy enough, so the team built a "System" button that contained everything important. The **psychological** barrier remained: nobody clicked it. Then they added one simple word: **"Start"**. Suddenly people clicked without fear.

<PostFigure
  src={figure6}
  alt="The Windows 95 desktop at first run"
  caption="User:Hohho56oy. Windows 95 at first run. Screenshot. Fair use."
/>

Nothing about the function had changed. The result was remarkable. Microsoft's insight was to turn behavioural science into design philosophy. It laid out a set of clear **verbs**: "Run", "Find", "Documents". For a lost user, this was a text-based, highly logical safety net. It did not offer a complicated tool. It offered a plain-language entrance.

## Dock

So what did Apple do? Apple was determined to make its operating system as polished as possible — so polished that Steve Jobs joked people would "want to lick it". At first glance, though, the interface looked chaotic next to Windows' clean, verb-driven structure.

Apple's answer was the **Dock**. Unlike the Start menu, the Dock is not a list. It is a **space** — a shelf that holds applications. It presents a set of **nouns**: "Finder", "Mail", "Photoshop". They are intuitive, concrete objects. Apple did not give users instructions. It gave them a space that felt like the physical world.

## Now

Fast-forward to today, and look at how we manage complex information. Tools such as Notion and Obsidian are increasingly popular, and their core idea is strict hierarchy and structure: pages inside pages, tags, databases. Why do we force thought into that rigid frame? The answer was written thirty years ago. It is the legacy of Windows — the **"system integration" mode**. It replaces the brain's natural associations with machine logic, and in exchange gives humans a superpower: handling more complexity than the brain can hold.

But what if the goal is not to organise what exists, but to **create**? Then we need a completely different mode: Miro, FigJam, Freeform. They offer an infinite canvas — no folders, no hierarchy, just elements scattered across it. An efficient, pure kind of mess. This is the continuation of Apple's philosophy: the **"blank canvas" mode**. It hides structure so the brain can wander through non-linear connections and stumble into new ideas.

So the old stereotype — "Mac for creatives, PC for business" — is not baseless. It is the result of two companies deliberately amplifying two different modes of thought: **one trains us to be a system, the other keeps us on a canvas**.

## Resolution

So why did the two companies never copy each other? Because **Start is not just a button**. Behind it sit the file system, the registry, and /Program Files — the visible tip of a huge hidden structure. And the **Dock** is not just a panel either. It expresses macOS's object-oriented philosophy: every app is a self-contained package that can be dragged anywhere.

To copy each other, either company would have to tear up its foundations and rebuild. That is also why technology so often feels "wrong". When you try to draw a messy mind map in a structured tool such as Excel, your opponent is not Excel itself but the philosophy underneath it. You are trying to paint on a canvas that was never meant for painting.

This is not the only case of a "simple design choice" hiding a deep philosophy. Depth, space, even "trust" can be reshaped through design. Blur, for instance, completely changed how we perceive hierarchy in a UI. At bottom, these stories are all **where technology, psychology, and art meet**.
